Physical AI & Security

A Quadruped Robot Can Now Badge Through Locked Doors on Its Own

Sept. 24, 2026 · ANYbotics, dormakaba & LEGIC

ANYbotics, dormakaba and LEGIC Identsystems have completed a pilot letting the four-legged ANYmal inspection robot open access-controlled doors by itself, carrying a secure digital credential the same way an employee's ID badge works. The pilot ran for several weeks at GE Vernova's Whitegate power plant in Ireland.

Until now, a locked door was often the one obstacle stopping an inspection robot from reaching hazardous or restricted areas. The partners are now preparing a commercial rollout and looking at extending the same idea to elevators and turnstiles.

Learn: what is a secure digital credential?

It is the electronic equivalent of a badge, an encrypted code a reader can check without letting anyone copy or fake it. Giving a robot its own credential, instead of just detecting its presence, means a facility's existing security system can log exactly when and where the robot went, the same audit trail it already keeps for people.

Electronics & Photonics

Morphotonics Raises €40M+ to Scale Optics for AI Glasses and Data Centers

Sept. 21–22, 2026 · Veldhoven, Netherlands

Dutch company Morphotonics closed a Series B round of more than €40 million to scale up its nanoimprint lithography machines, which stamp optical patterns onto materials instead of using light to etch them the way chipmaking lithography does. Its main product today is waveguides, the transparent parts inside AR and smart glasses that steer a digital image into the wearer's eye.

The new funding will also push Morphotonics into co-packaged optics, components that move data as light between chips inside an AI data center, which need far less power than moving the same data as electrical signals.

Learn: nanoimprint vs. photolithography

Chip fabs use light through a mask to draw circuits (photolithography). Nanoimprint lithography instead presses a physical stamp into a soft material, like using a mould. It cannot draw features as small as the most advanced chips need, but it is fast and cheap over large areas, which suits optical components like waveguides and data-center light-links.

Semiconductor Industry

Tim Cook Wins the Chip Industry's Highest Honor, the Noyce Award

Sept. 22, 2026 · Semiconductor Industry Association

The Semiconductor Industry Association named Apple Executive Chair Tim Cook as the 2026 recipient of the Robert N. Noyce Award, the industry's top honor, citing his role in advancing U.S. semiconductor manufacturing and design. He will receive it at the SIA Awards Dinner on November 19, 2026, in San Jose.

The award is named for Robert Noyce, who co-founded Intel and co-invented the integrated circuit. Cook stepped down as Apple's CEO on September 1, 2026, after fifteen years, and now serves as executive chair.

Learn: who was Robert Noyce, and why does the award matter?

Robert Noyce co-invented the integrated circuit, the technology that put many transistors on a single chip and made every modern electronic device possible, then co-founded Intel. The award recognizes leadership that benefits the whole chip industry, not just one company, so its recipients are usually a good shortlist of who is currently shaping how and where chips get designed and made.

Jargon Buster
Depth camera
A camera that measures distance to objects, not just color and shape.
ROS
Robot Operating System: shared tools that let a robot's parts talk to each other.
MoveIt
Open-source software that plans safe, collision-free arm movements.
TOPS
Trillion operations per second, a measure of AI chip speed.
Secure credential
An encrypted digital ID a reader can verify without it being copied.
Nanoimprint lithography
Stamping a pattern into material instead of etching it with light.
Co-packaged optics
Light-based links placed right next to a chip to move data faster and cooler.
Integrated circuit
Many transistors built together on one chip.
